In recent years, food delivery applications have become an increasingly visible part of
urban life in Dhaka. Platforms such as Foodpanda and Pathao Food promise quick,
convenient access to meals from a wide range of restaurants. For many university
students and young professionals, ordering food through an app has become a routine
solution to busy schedules, traffic congestion, and the desire to avoid crowded
restaurants. However, the rapid growth of this industry also raises important social and
economic questions about working conditions, consumer behavior, and the future of
local food businesses.
One of the main attractions of food delivery apps is convenience. Users can browse
menus, compare prices, read reviews, and place orders without leaving their homes or
hostels. For students living near campuses in areas like Dhanmondi, Mirpur, or
Bashundhara, these services can save significant time, especially during exam periods or
project deadlines. Some users also argue that delivery apps allow them to maintain a
more flexible daily routine, since they no longer need to plan meals around fixed
canteen hours or long queues.
At the same time, the business model behind these platforms relies heavily on a large
workforce of delivery riders. Many riders work long hours, often late into the night, to
meet customer demand and earn incentives. Reports have highlighted issues such as
low base pay, dependence on tips, lack of formal employment benefits, and pressure to
complete deliveries quickly. Traffic accidents involving delivery riders have also become
a matter of public concern. While companies describe riders as “partners” or
“independent contractors,” critics argue that this language masks the reality of
precarious, low-security work.
The impact on local restaurants is another contested issue. On one hand, small and
medium restaurants gain access to a wider customer base without needing to invest in
their own delivery system. On the other hand, platforms charge significant commissions
on each order, which can reduce profit margins. Some restaurant owners report feeling
forced to join multiple apps to remain competitive, even as they worry about long-term
sustainability. There is also a risk that popular chains with bigger marketing budgets will
dominate app visibility, pushing smaller eateries further to the margins.
Consumer behavior is changing as well. Easy access to restaurant food may reduce
home cooking and shared family meals, especially among young people living in rented
flats or university halls. Nutritionists warn that frequent consumption of restaurant food,
which is often high in oil, salt, and sugar, could contribute to health problems over time.
At the same time, some users say that delivery apps allow them to explore diverse
cuisines and support local businesses they might not have discovered otherwise.
The story of food delivery apps in Dhaka is not simply about technology or convenience.
It reflects broader tensions between rapid digitalization, labor rights, public health, and
the changing culture of urban eating. As these platforms continue to expand, the
choices made by consumers, companies, and policymakers will shape not only how
people eat, but also how work is organized and valued in the city’s growing digital
economy.
