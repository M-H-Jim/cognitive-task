Multitasking, Attention, and the Role of Artificial Intelligence

University students increasingly perform academic activities while simultaneously interacting with digital technologies. A student may read a textbook while receiving messages, monitor notifications while completing an assignment, or use an artificial intelligence (AI) system while studying. Although people often describe themselves as effective multitaskers, cognitive psychology suggests that the human capacity to process information is limited. When several activities compete for attention, individuals may experience changes in accuracy, speed, memory, and mental effort.

Attention is the cognitive process that allows people to select information for further processing. At any moment, individuals encounter more information than they can consciously process in detail. Selective attention helps them concentrate on information that is relevant to their current goal while filtering out competing stimuli. However, attention has limited capacity. When a person must respond to an additional stimulus while completing a primary task, some cognitive resources must be allocated away from the primary activity. This division of resources can affect performance.

Reading an academic article illustrates these demands. Effective reading requires more than recognizing individual words. Readers must identify important ideas, connect information across paragraphs, distinguish major arguments from supporting details, and maintain previously encountered information in memory. Working memory plays an important role in this process. Working memory is a limited cognitive system that temporarily stores and manipulates information. For example, when reading an argument, a student may need to remember an earlier claim while interpreting evidence presented several paragraphs later.

A secondary task can interfere with these processes. Consider a student who is reading an article while monitoring numbers appearing on a computer screen. The student may need to shift attention from the article to the number, determine whether it is relevant, and make an appropriate response before returning to the article. Even if each interruption lasts only a short period, repeated interruptions can increase the demands placed on attention and working memory. The student may miss information in the article or require additional time to reconstruct the context after each interruption.

Multitasking can therefore produce a performance cost. One explanation is that people do not truly perform all tasks simultaneously; instead, they rapidly shift attention between competing activities. Each shift requires the individual to identify the current task, retrieve relevant information, and select an appropriate response. Frequent switching may increase mental effort and reduce efficiency. The effects may be particularly noticeable when both tasks require active information processing rather than simple automatic responses.

Cognitive load provides another way of understanding these effects. Cognitive load refers to the amount of mental effort required while performing a task. Some load comes from the complexity of the material itself, while additional load can arise from distractions or inefficient task demands. When cognitive load becomes high, fewer resources may remain available for maintaining and manipulating information in working memory. As a result, individuals may find it more difficult to understand complex material, remember details, or respond accurately to secondary stimuli.

Generative AI introduces a new factor into this situation. Tools such as ChatGPT and Gemini can explain unfamiliar concepts, identify important ideas, organize information, and provide feedback. These functions may reduce some of the effort required to process complex academic material. For instance, a student who does not understand a theoretical concept may ask an AI system for a simpler explanation. The student can then use that explanation to support further understanding of the original material.

However, AI assistance does not automatically reduce cognitive demands. Effective use of an AI system requires the user to formulate an appropriate question, evaluate the response, and determine whether the information is relevant and accurate. The user may also need to switch between the academic material and the AI interface. Consequently, AI can potentially function as either a cognitive support or an additional source of distraction, depending on how it is used.

The effects of AI may therefore differ from the effects of multitasking alone. A student using AI during a demanding task might experience lower perceived cognitive load if the technology helps organize information efficiently. On the other hand, frequent interaction with AI could increase attentional switching and introduce additional demands. The outcome may depend on the nature of the task, the amount of AI interaction, and the student's ability to evaluate and integrate AI-generated information.

Understanding these relationships is increasingly important in university education. Academic work now occurs in environments where students can easily access multiple digital resources at the same time. Studying how multitasking influences attention, working memory, and cognitive load can help clarify the costs of divided attention. Examining AI-assisted multitasking may further reveal whether generative AI helps students manage competing cognitive demands or creates additional demands of its own. Such research can contribute to a better understanding of how students learn and perform in technology-rich academic environments.
